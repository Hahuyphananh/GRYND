# GRYND Video Editor

## Core Editing Philosophy

1. Act like a professional short-form gaming video editor, NOT a subtitle generator.
2. The goal is entertainment, retention, and making the gameplay feel exciting.
3. Never assume that adding text alone makes an edit good.
4. Every edit should have intentional visual changes when they improve the moment:
   - cuts
   - punch-in zooms
   - zoom-outs
   - speed changes
   - emphasis
   - motion
   - screen effects
   - transitions
   - sound effects
   - music when appropriate
5. Do not add effects randomly. Effects must reinforce what is happening in the gameplay.
6. Aggressively remove dead time, waiting, repetitive actions, menus, and uninteresting moments.
7. Analyze the gameplay BEFORE editing and identify the strongest moment, tension-building moments, and payoff.
8. Prefer a simple but highly intentional edit over an edit overloaded with effects.
9. The viewer should understand what is happening even if they know nothing about GRYND.
10. The first 1-2 seconds must create curiosity or tension.
11. Build a structure where possible:
    HOOK → SETUP/TENSION → ACTION → PAYOFF → CTA
12. Captions must be visually prominent and readable on a phone. Never create tiny subtitles that disappear into the gameplay.
13. Important gameplay events should receive stronger visual emphasis than ordinary moments.
14. Editing decisions should be based on the actual gameplay rather than blindly applying the same timestamps/effects to every video.
15. Optimize the final result specifically for TikTok, Instagram Reels, and YouTube Shorts in 9:16.
16. The final result should feel like a human gaming creator/editor made it.

**Quality over quantity: If an effect does not noticeably improve viewer retention, clarity, emotion, or emphasis, do not use it.**

## Visual Editing Rules

### Pacing

* Remove pauses, waiting, hesitation, menus, and anything that does not contribute to the story.
* Remove repetitive actions unless they build humor or tension.
* Prefer purposeful, tight shots.
* Do NOT force a 15-20 second duration if the best story needs slightly more or less time.
* Keep the viewer moving toward the payoff.
* Make cuts at meaningful gameplay events, not randomly.

### Dynamic framing

Use visual movement to make important moments feel important:

* Punch-in zoom on critical actions, decisions, wins, losses, near-misses, and surprising events.
* Return to normal framing after the emphasis.
* Use stronger zooms for major moments and subtle zooms for minor moments.
* Use gradual push-ins to build tension.
* Use quick punch-ins for sudden events.
* Keep the gameplay readable at all times.
* Never zoom so far that important gameplay UI becomes unreadable.

### Speed and timing

When appropriate:

* Speed up boring sections.
* Slow down or briefly hold on a major moment.
* Use speed changes to build anticipation before a payoff.
* Return to normal speed when the important action happens.
* Do not use speed changes if they make the gameplay confusing or hard to follow.

### Visual emphasis

When something important happens, consider:

* zoom
* brief freeze/hold
* screen shake
* flash/impact effect
* motion
* rapid cut
* visual highlight

Only use these when they reinforce the event and make it feel more important to the viewer.

### Captions and text

Captions must be impossible to miss on a phone:

* Use large, high-contrast, easy-to-read text.
* Keep text within safe areas.
* Use short, punchy phrases.
* Use animated entrance/exit if supported.
* Add emphasis to important words.
* Show text exactly when it is relevant, and remove it when it is not.
* Never place captions over important gameplay info.
* Never create tiny centered subtitles that disappear into the gameplay.
* Hooks should visually stand out from ordinary captions.
* CTA text should be prominent and readable.

### Hooks

The first 1-2 seconds are critical. The hook should create curiosity, tension, or a challenge:

* challenge the viewer
* show the beginning of a clutch moment
* tease an outcome
* create uncertainty
* ask a question (e.g., "THINK YOU CAN WIN?")
* immediately show an unusual event

Do not use the exact same hook on every video. Match it to the gameplay.

### Sound design

When audio tools are available:

* Use sound effects to reinforce major actions and transitions.
* Use impact sounds for wins, losses, reveals, and sudden events.
* Use whooshes for movement and speed changes.
* Use music when it improves pacing and does not overpower the gameplay.
* Keep audio balanced so the gameplay sounds clear.
* Do not add random sound effects just to seem edited.

### Storytelling

Where possible, structure the video:

HOOK → SETUP/TENSION → ACTION → PAYOFF → CTA

The strongest gameplay moment should generally be the payoff.

### Professionalism

The final result should NOT feel like:

* raw gameplay with subtitles
* gameplay with random zooms
* a slideshow of effects
* a subtitle generator

It should feel like a professional creator intentionally edited the gameplay to maximize retention and excitement.

> Every visual or audio edit must have a reason. The editor should be able to explain why each major cut, zoom, speed change, caption, effect, and sound was used.

The detailed editing workflow (window selection, text overlay placement, and save verification) will be added here in a later step.

## Editorial Standards

These rules adapt the short-form editorial principles from the video-editing skill to the CapCut MCP workflow. They refine the Visual Editing Rules above without changing how the timeline is built. They add **no** render engine or code-based editing dependency — everything still runs inside the CapCut draft through the CapCut MCP.

### 1. Engineered hooks

A hook that merely describes the clip is not a hook. The first 1-2 seconds must create curiosity, tension, or a competitive challenge.

* Avoid generic descriptive captions (e.g. "Watch this blackjack round").
* Use short, punchy phrases that raise a question or stake a claim.
* GRYND examples: "DOWN 0-1." / "ONE CARD DECIDES IT." / "WOULD YOU HIT?" / "THIS ROUND CHANGES EVERYTHING." / "CAN YOU CLUTCH THIS?"
* Pair the hook text with a visual treatment where the MCP supports it: a framing change, a fast opening cut, motion, or at minimum strong hook text.
* Never use a hook unless it matches the actual gameplay. A hook that overpromises what the clip shows is misleading.

### 2. Non-metronomic pacing

Do not cut on a fixed clock. A predictable rhythm reads as template editing.

* Do NOT cut every 1 second or follow a metronomic rhythm.
* Cut based on gameplay events: decisions, card/result reveals, reactions, rising tension, and the payoff.
* Shorten cuts as tension increases; let important moments breathe when necessary.
* Remove dead time aggressively — waiting, menus, repetitive actions.
* Two clips must never be paced identically just because they have the same length.

### 3. Caption emphasis ladder

Adapted for the CapCut MCP. Captions are a hierarchy, not a transcript:

* **NORMAL** — readable supporting text: legible, restrained, and clear of gameplay UI.
* **IMPORTANT** — larger/bolder treatment or stronger contrast at meaningful moments.
* **HIGHLIGHT** — visually dominant treatment for the key word or result (the winning hand, the cash-out, the crash number).

Rules:

* Use keyword emphasis rather than making every word equally prominent: one accent word or phrase leads, the rest support.
* Captions should look like designed short-form gaming graphics, NOT subtitles.
* Prefer high-contrast treatments that remain readable over gameplay.
* Never use plain white text over bright gameplay without a strong contrast treatment.
* If the MCP does not support true caption animation, approximate emphasis through timing, size, placement, layering, or other supported properties.

### 4. Visual-first editing

Captions alone are NOT an edit. Every short should intentionally use some combination of:

* meaningful cuts
* punch-in framing
* scale changes
* pacing changes
* gameplay emphasis
* visual hierarchy
* timing changes
* supported SFX/audio emphasis
* transitions/effects only when supported and justified

The result must still feel edited if all captions were removed.

### 5. Editing intensity

Create three levels and keep them distinct:

* **NORMAL** — minimal movement: clean, readable gameplay.
* **IMPORTANT** — noticeable emphasis: framing change, stronger caption, motion.
* **MAJOR** — strongest visual treatment for the payoff/clutch/result.

Do not apply the MAJOR treatment to everything. Contrast between the levels is what makes MAJOR land.

### 6. Camera / zoom discipline

* Zooms must be tied to gameplay events.
* Avoid random or constant zooming.
* Avoid tiny changes that are imperceptible to viewers.
* Prefer clearly visible but controlled punch-ins.
* If future keyframe support becomes available, prefer eased camera movement rather than linear/jittery movement.
* With current CapCut MCP limitations, use supported scale/cut techniques instead — a clean cut combined with a segment scale change reads as a punch-in.

### 7. Audio / SFX

Audio supports the edit rather than becoming noise.

* Preserve useful original gameplay audio (cards, chips, results).
* Use SFX only at meaningful moments (reveals, wins, losses, transitions).
* Prefer one intentional audio layer/track where practical over stacking competing sounds.
* If useful, synthesized FFmpeg SFX may be created externally and then added through the CapCut MCP.
* Never claim SFX were added unless they actually exist in the project — verify in the timeline.

### 8. Short-form editing checklist

Before considering an edit finished, verify:

* Strong hook in the first 1-2 seconds
* No unnecessary dead time
* Pacing varies naturally
* Gameplay is always understandable
* Captions are readable and visually designed
* Important moments receive stronger emphasis
* Payoff is obvious
* CTA does not interrupt the payoff
* 9:16 framing is correct
* The video would still look intentionally edited without captions
* No unsupported CapCut features are claimed
* Final timeline is structurally verified

### 9. Anti-patterns

Explicitly forbidden:

* subtitle-only edits
* identical caption styling throughout
* metronomic 1-second cuts
* random zoom spam
* microscopic visual effects
* effects unrelated to gameplay
* covering important gameplay UI
* white captions disappearing into bright backgrounds
* excessive text
* generic hooks disconnected from the actual clip
* adding effects just to make the edit appear "busy"

## Gameplay Analysis Workflow

The editor must perform a gameplay analysis BEFORE making any timeline modifications.

### 1. Inspect first

Before editing:

* Identify the source CapCut project and available gameplay media.
* Read the timeline, clip durations, tracks, and available media information using the CapCut MCP.
* Determine the actual gameplay window available.
* Never assume fixed timestamps.

### 2. Identify moments

Analyze the gameplay and identify:

* strongest/highest-interest moments
* wins
* losses
* near-misses
* risky decisions
* surprising outcomes
* major changes in the game state
* moments of tension
* moments that could create curiosity
* boring/dead sections that should be removed

### 3. Choose the story

Select the strongest possible short-form story from the available footage.

Prefer:

* an immediately interesting opening
* a clear situation
* escalating tension
* a meaningful decision/action
* a satisfying or surprising payoff

Do NOT simply take the middle 15 seconds or the first 15 seconds unless that is genuinely the strongest section.

### 4. Rank moments

Internally rank candidate moments by:

1. viewer curiosity
2. tension
3. visual clarity
4. payoff strength
5. uniqueness
6. potential for visual/audio emphasis

Use the highest-quality combination rather than simply choosing the longest or most active section.

### 5. Plan before editing

Before making timeline changes, create an internal edit plan containing:

* selected source section(s)
* intended final duration
* hook
* setup
* tension-building moments
* payoff
* CTA
* planned cuts
* planned zooms/emphasis
* planned speed changes
* planned captions
* planned sound effects/music if available

The plan must be based on the actual gameplay.

### 6. Edit only after analysis

Do not modify the timeline until the gameplay analysis and edit plan are complete.

### 7. Adapt to the game

The editing strategy should change depending on the GRYND game.

For example:

* Blackjack: emphasize risky decisions, card reveals, busts, wins, and close outcomes.
* Roulette PvP: emphasize predictions, tension before the result, and the result itself.
* Crash PvP: emphasize rising tension, player decisions, cash-out/fold moments, and the crash/payoff.
* Plinko: emphasize anticipation, trajectory, near-misses, and the final result.
* Other GRYND games: identify the equivalent decision, tension, and payoff moments instead of copying another game's editing pattern.

### 8. Never invent events

Only describe or emphasize events that actually occur in the footage.

Do not create fake gameplay outcomes, fake reactions, or misleading captions.

### 9. Final selection

If several moments are good, choose the one with the strongest combination of:

HOOK → TENSION → PAYOFF.

Add this final principle:

> Analyze first, edit second. The timeline should be shaped around the gameplay's story, not the other way around.

## CapCut Implementation Rules

### 1. Use real CapCut capabilities

Before attempting an effect, transition, animation, filter, speed change, or audio treatment, inspect the available CapCut MCP tools/capabilities.

* Only use effects and operations that are actually supported by the available CapCut MCP.
* Do not pretend an effect was applied if the MCP did not successfully apply it.
* If a requested effect is unsupported, use the closest supported alternative that produces a clearly visible result.

### 2. Make visual changes actually visible

The final edit must contain meaningful visual changes beyond captions.

A technically applied effect is not sufficient if it is effectively invisible to the viewer.

Prefer edits with an obvious perceptual impact when they improve the moment:

* noticeable punch-in zooms
* dynamic framing
* speed ramps or speed changes
* brief holds/freezes when supported
* impactful transitions
* visual emphasis
* motion
* screen effects
* appropriately timed overlays

Avoid microscopic changes that technically modify the timeline but cannot reasonably be noticed on a phone.

### 3. Match effects to gameplay events

Every major effect must correspond to a real event in the gameplay.

Examples:

* card reveal → punch-in / emphasis
* winning hand → stronger visual emphasis
* bust → impact treatment
* near miss → brief tension effect
* major decision → gradual push-in
* crash approaching → increasing visual intensity
* crash/result → strongest emphasis
* roulette result → anticipation followed by result emphasis

Do not add effects simply to make the timeline look busy.

### 4. Use layered editing

When appropriate, combine multiple editing techniques around important moments.

For example:

* cut + punch-in + caption emphasis
* speed-up + transition + impact sound
* gradual zoom + tension caption + music build
* result hold + brief freeze + payoff

Do not stack effects automatically. Layer them only when they strengthen the story.

### 5. Effect intensity hierarchy

Use different levels of intensity:

SUBTLE:

* small framing adjustment
* gentle push-in
* minor caption animation

MEDIUM:

* noticeable punch-in
* stronger caption animation
* speed change
* transition
* moderate visual emphasis

MAJOR:

* strongest punch-in
* major impact treatment
* brief hold/freeze
* strong transition
* major caption emphasis
* strong sound effect

Reserve MAJOR treatment for the most important gameplay moments.

### 6. Avoid effect overload

* Do not put an effect on every cut.
* Do not zoom constantly.
* Do not use the same effect repeatedly without purpose.
* Allow normal footage to breathe between emphasized moments.
* Visual contrast between normal gameplay and emphasized moments makes important moments feel more important.

### 7. Timeline verification

After making edits:

* Re-read the modified CapCut timeline using the MCP.
* Verify that intended cuts, overlays, timing, and effects actually exist.
* Verify that important effects overlap the intended gameplay moments.
* Verify that text is visible for the intended duration.
* Verify that the final duration is appropriate.
* If possible, inspect or render a preview to confirm the result is visually meaningful.
* If an effect was not successfully applied, do not claim that it was.

### 8. Prefer editing over explanation

The goal is to produce an actual professional-looking CapCut edit.

Do not compensate for unsupported editing operations by writing a long explanation that the effect could not be applied.

Instead:

1. Detect the limitation.
2. Choose a supported alternative.
3. Apply it.
4. Verify it.
5. Continue editing.

### 9. Preserve source media

* Never modify or overwrite the original gameplay recording.
* Make all changes inside the CapCut draft/project.
* Preserve the original source media and its integrity.

> A professional edit is not defined by how many operations were performed. It is defined by whether the viewer can clearly feel the pacing, emphasis, tension, and payoff.

## GRYND Meme Reel Mode

### 1. Definition

GRYND Meme Reel Mode is a dedicated editing mode for **silent GRYND gameplay footage**: gameplay video plus game audio only, with no commentary, no spoken dialogue, and no existing captions burned in.

The goal is NOT to create subtitles. The goal is to transform the raw gameplay into a polished 15–20 second competitive-gaming Reel organized around ONE original meme-style editorial headline.

The mode is activated when the user explicitly asks for a meme-style or meme-reel GRYND edit (for example "make this a meme reel", "turn this into a meme", "meme-style edit"). When activated, it **overrides the generic hook/CTA behavior** of the normal GRYND video mode below.

### 1.1 Skill is the source of truth

Before editing ANY GRYND video, the agent MUST read and follow `.opencode/skills/grynd-video-editor/SKILL.md`.

These rules are mandatory, not suggestions.

The agent must NOT improvise a different caption/layout implementation simply because a technically possible CapCut operation exists.

### 2. Mode entry criteria

Only use this mode when ALL of the following are true:

* The footage is GRYND gameplay.
* The footage contains game audio only.
* There is no commentary, no spoken dialogue, and no narration.
* There are no existing captions or subtitles in the footage.

If the footage contains speech or existing captions, do not use this mode — use the normal GRYND video mode. Never run speech transcription for this mode; these clips contain no speech.

### 3. Story-first analysis

The caption is generated **only after the gameplay has been visually analyzed**. The AI must look at what actually happens before writing anything.

Analyze the footage and identify the story using this structure:

1. **SETUP** — the situation the player is in.
2. **CONFLICT/TENSION** — the uncertainty or pressure in the round.
3. **DECISION/ACTION** — the risky or meaningful thing the player does.
4. **PAYOFF** — the result: win, loss, bust, near-miss, surprise.

The strongest available 15–20 second story is selected from this analysis. Start the Reel directly in the interesting conflict/tension — do not open with menu dead time or generic waiting.

### 4. The meme headline

Generate **ONE original meme-style editorial headline** that describes or enhances the actual story identified above.

Writing rules:

* The headline is a **persistent editorial headline**, NEVER subtitles.
* It should be humorous, competitive, ironic, surprising, or curiosity-driven when appropriate.
* Base it **strictly on what actually happens** in the footage. Never invent events.
* Keep it short and punchy — one readable line at most.
* Never split it into word-by-word or sentence-fragment captions.
* Emojis such as 💀 are acceptable when they reinforce the joke, but are not required.

The following are examples of caption **STYLE only**. They are examples of voice and tone, and **must never be blindly reused**. Generate a headline that matches the actual gameplay:

* "bro really thought 16 was enough 💀"
* "this was NOT part of the plan"
* "one decision ruined everything"
* "ain't no way he picked THAT"

Before committing to a headline, validate it against the footage: if the headline describes an event that is not visible in the clip, rewrite it. Do not reuse examples from this list verbatim.

### 5. Persistent headline implementation

The headline is a major visual design element and must be implemented as persistent editorial text:

* Place it on a **dedicated top text track** layered above the gameplay.
* Give it one **large, centered, intentional safe-area position** — never placed randomly.
* Use strong contrast so it stays readable on every frame where it appears.
* Target approximately **70–95 font size on a 1080×1920 canvas**, adjusted based on the actual composition.
* If the template text styling cannot guarantee readability over bright gameplay, build a reliable outline/drop-shadow effect using **layered text copies on separate, distinct tracks**: 2–4 darker copies of the identical text, offset by a few pixels on lower tracks, with the main light-colored copy on top. Overlapping segments must never share a single track (validated as an overlap); each layer needs its own track.
* Keep the headline visible across **most or all** of the Reel. A brief absence is allowed only if the story specifically benefits from it.
* Never allow the headline to obscure important gameplay UI — compute its position so the game readout stays clear.
* Keep styling consistent across the Reel: same size, same color, same position for the persistent headline.

A separate **keyword emphasis/burst** near the payoff is allowed, but it is one designed accent element, NOT a series of subtitle lines. Never turn the headline into fragments.

### 5.1 Caption layers must look like ONE caption

The final render must contain **one visually readable headline**, never multiple copies of the same headline.

* Layered text is allowed ONLY when the layers visually combine into a single outline/stroke/shadow effect.
* Never create visibly separated duplicate copies of the same caption.
* If CapCut supports a reliable native text outline/stroke property, prefer that over duplicated text layers.
* If duplicated layers are necessary:
  * use the minimum offset required for the outline/shadow
  * keep all copies aligned precisely
  * ensure they visually merge into one glyph
  * never use large offsets
  * never allow the shadow copies to read as separate captions

Apply this rule to both the persistent headline and payoff text such as `BUST.`.

### 5.2 Caption positioning must be verified from CapCut's coordinate system

Never blindly reuse a `posY` value from another CapCut draft.

* Before adding text, inspect the existing text template/transform conventions and determine how CapCut's normalized text coordinates map to the actual 1080×1920 canvas.
* The persistent headline must:
  * have `posX = 0` (or the equivalent true horizontal center)
  * be positioned in the upper safe area
  * be visually centered at the top
  * remain clear of important gameplay UI
  * not be placed merely because a numeric coordinate "looks reasonable"
* The same principle applies to payoff text.

### 6. Gameplay editing

The gameplay carries the motion and energy. Apply the same visual editing discipline as the normal mode with these mode-specific rules:

* Select the strongest 15–20 second story from the actual footage.
* Start directly in the conflict/tension.
* Use **event-based cuts** keyed to the story beats, never fixed one-second intervals.
* Use **clearly visible cut-to-scale punch-ins** (split + scale change) at important moments.
* Increase visual intensity as the Reel approaches the payoff.
* **Hold briefly** on the payoff when a hold strengthens the moment.
* Preserve the gameplay audio.
* Use SFX only if actually available through supported CapCut functionality (for example a real audio template in the draft). Never claim unsupported effects.
* Do not use random zooms or random effects.
* Do not use ASCII-art or title-card hacks.
* Do not invent gameplay events.

### 7. Workflow

Run this exact sequence for every meme reel:

1. **ANALYZE** the gameplay visually (read the timeline; extract and inspect frames at the key moments).
2. **Identify the story** — SETUP → CONFLICT/TENSION → DECISION/ACTION → PAYOFF.
3. **Generate the meme concept** — one original editorial headline from the actual events.
4. **Validate the concept against the footage** — confirm the headline matches what actually happens.
5. **Select the 15–20 second window** from the strongest story.
6. **Build the gameplay edit** — event-based cuts, cut-to-scale punch-ins, pacing toward the payoff, hold on the payoff.
7. **Build the persistent headline** — dedicated top text track, one large centered safe-area headline, high-contrast layered treatment.
8. **Add payoff emphasis** — the single keyword emphasis/burst near the payoff.
9. **TEXT QA** — after text is added, run the mandatory text implementation check below, and FIX the text before proceeding if it fails:
   * inspect every text segment
   * identify duplicate headline layers
   * verify shadow/outline layers visually converge into one glyph
   * verify no same-track text overlap
   * verify horizontal centering
   * verify upper-safe-area positioning
   * verify gameplay UI is not obscured
   * verify the headline remains readable
10. **Verify the timeline** — re-read the draft, confirm cuts, punch-ins, headline placement, and the emphasis burst actually exist.
11. **Save** the draft through the CapCut MCP after verification.

### 8. Mode-specific anti-patterns

Explicitly forbidden in GRYND Meme Reel Mode:

* subtitle-like caption fragments derived from the headline
* word-by-word or sentence-splitting of the headline
* random or metronomic one-second cuts
* random zoom spam or effects unrelated to gameplay
* generic hook text copied onto every video
* ASCII-art / title-card text hacks
* invented gameplay events or misleading headlines
* placing the headline over important gameplay UI
* claiming SFX or effects that are not actually in the project
* adding rendering engines (HyperFrames, Remotion, or any other external renderer) — all work happens inside the CapCut draft through the CapCut MCP

### 9. Final memory

> In Meme Reel Mode the headline is the point of view, but the gameplay is the joke. One persistent line, real events only, and an edit that makes the payoff land.

### 9.1 Non-negotiable proven rules

These rules worked in live testing and MUST be kept on every future GRYND Meme Reel edit:

* 15–20 second story-driven edit
* event-based cuts
* cut-to-scale punch-ins
* rising intensity toward the payoff
* persistent editorial headline
* gameplay audio preserved
* no generic CTA
* no invented gameplay events
* no speech transcription
* no HyperFrames/Remotion rendering engine

### 10. Test feedback / failure learning

Real rendered-output feedback takes priority when refining the implementation.

First observed failures from live testing (2026-09-12, real GRYND blackjack meme reel):

* duplicated caption layers can appear as separate visible captions
* assumed normalized `posY` values can produce incorrect visual placement
* timeline validation alone does NOT prove visual correctness
* text implementation must be treated as a visual composition problem, not merely a valid-timeline problem
